# Benchmarks: Runtime, False Alarms and Coverage

Fifty methods share one interface in this package, which makes it easy
to swap one for another and easy to forget that they do not cost the
same thing. This article answers three questions a reader choosing a
method has, from the measurements that ship with the package:

1.  **How long does it take**, and at what series length does it stop
    being usable at all?
2.  **How often does it fire when there is nothing there?**
3.  **Do the intervals cover?**

## How these numbers were produced, and what they are not

The tables come from the `cpt_runtimes`, `cpt_null_sizes` and
`cpt_calibration` data sets, generated by the scripts in `data-raw/` for
this release on one Linux x86-64 machine (AMD EPYC) with R 4.4.1 and the
engine versions current then, and read here when the page is built. The
runtimes are **one fit per cell**: they answer “will it finish?”, and
are not a basis for saying one engine is some factor faster than
another. Re-measure on your own hardware before quoting a time.

## 1. Runtime

Simulated Gaussian series with four mean changes of two noise standard
deviations (three coordinates for the multivariate engines), each fit in
its own R process after a warm-up call, with a two-minute cap (five at a
million). A length is attempted only when the previous one finished in
under ten seconds.

``` r

rt <- cpt_runtimes
rt$cell <- ifelse(rt$status == "ok", format(round(rt$seconds, 2), nsmall = 2),
                  rt$status)
wide <- reshape(as.data.frame(rt[, c("method", "n", "cell")]),
                idvar = "method", timevar = "n", direction = "wide")
names(wide) <- c("method", "1e3", "1e4", "1e5", "1e6")[seq_along(wide)]
wide[is.na(wide)] <- ""
fastest <- rt[rt$n == 1e6 & rt$status == "ok", ]
# Fastest at a million first, then the rest alphabetically.
rank_1e6 <- match(wide$method, fastest$method[order(fastest$seconds)])
wide <- wide[order(is.na(rank_1e6), rank_1e6, wide$method), ]
knitr::kable(wide, row.names = FALSE,
             caption = "Seconds per fit (blank: not attempted)")
```

15 methods fit a million points inside the cap. The pruned
optimal-partitioning family and the single-change test are fast enough
that length is not a consideration; the random-interval searches cost
more because they fit many sub-intervals by construction, which is also
why they find short changes the single-pass methods miss. The methods
that stop at 1,000 or 10,000 are quadratic or worse in the length
(`ecp`, `bocpd`, `segneigh`’s cost matrix), solve a harder problem
(`cpop`’s continuous piecewise-linear fit), or were written for the
lengths their fields see (`strucchange`, `taylor`). None is broken; they
are methods for short series, and choosing one for a long series is a
modelling decision rather than a performance problem to tune around.
[`cpt_methods()`](https://pursuitofdatascience.github.io/ggchangepoint/reference/cpt_methods.md)
carries each method’s measured `cost` and `max_n`, and
`cpt_recommend(n = )` leaves out the ones that did not finish at the
length you give it.

## 2. False alarms on pure noise

Independent Gaussian noise with no changepoint anywhere, for the
univariate engines the runtime table shows finishing each length
quickly: 50 replicates at 1,000, 10 at 10,000 and 6 at 100,000. Every
changepoint reported is a false alarm.

``` r
ns <- cpt_null_sizes

wide_ns <- reshape(as.data.frame(ns[, c("method", "n", "cell")]),
                   idvar = "method", timevar = "n", direction = "wide")
names(wide_ns) <- c("method", "1e3", "1e4", "1e5")[seq_along(wide_ns)]
```

Most engines raise none at their defaults. The rows that do are worth
reading correctly:

- **`segmented`** always reports the breakpoint it is asked for
  (`npsi = 1`), change or no change; test it with
  [`cpt_test()`](https://pursuitofdatascience.github.io/ggchangepoint/reference/cpt_test.md)
  before reporting it.
- **`smuce`** and **`hsmuce`** default to `alpha = 0.5`, the level that
  is optimal for estimating the number of changes, and which by design
  overestimates it in up to half of all series. Pass a smaller `alpha`
  for a test.
- **`cpm`** is a sequential monitor used in batch mode: its threshold is
  a false-alarm rate per observation, so it reports about `n / arl0`
  false changepoints on any series. Since 0.6.0 the default `arl0`
  scales with the length (five times `n`, up to the 50,000 cpm ships),
  and the result records the expected count.
- **`np`** over-detects on Gaussian noise, and more so as the series
  grows.

Zero false alarms in a handful of replicates is consistent with a true
rate anywhere below a few tenths per series, so a zero here means “not
obviously anti-conservative” rather than “exact”. And it is measured on
independent noise: under autocorrelation almost every one of those zeros
becomes a positive count (`cpt_noise_benchmark`, and the pitfalls
article).

## 3. Interval coverage

The calibration article has the measured coverage of each provenance of
[`cpt_confint()`](https://pursuitofdatascience.github.io/ggchangepoint/reference/cpt_confint.md):

``` r

subset(cpt_calibration, kind == "coverage")[, c("call", "nominal",
                                                 "realised", "mcse")]
#> # A tibble: 3 × 4
#>   call                                                 nominal realised   mcse
#>   <chr>                                                  <dbl>    <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 "cpt_detect(x, method = \"strucchange\")"               0.95    0.953 0.0047
#> 2 "cpt_detect(x, method = \"smuce\", alpha = 0.05)"       0.95    1     0.0005
#> 3 "cpt_confint(cpt_detect(x), method = \"bootstrap\")"    0.95    0.968 0.0056
```

Two provenances are not in it yet. **NSP’s regions** hold a *global*
guarantee across all intervals and survive selection, so they are
conservative by design. **Posterior intervals** from `bcp` can be close
to vacuous at 95%: its posterior probability of a change is sharply
peaked but has diffuse tails, so pushing the credible level out sweeps
in most of the series.
[`cpt_confint()`](https://pursuitofdatascience.github.io/ggchangepoint/reference/cpt_confint.md)
warns when that happens, and its `source` column says which provenance
each interval has, because a posterior credible interval and a
confidence interval are not interchangeable.
